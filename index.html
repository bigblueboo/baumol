A playground for Baumol's cost disease

One hour is still one hour

Since 2000, the price of a television has fallen by about 97 percent. The price of an hour of childcare has more than doubled. Same country, same dollars, same decades. This page is about why. By the end, you will have caused the whole thing yourself.

Two kinds of prices

Sort what people buy by what its price has done since 2000, and the list splits into two clean piles. Anything a machine can copy or multiply got cheaper, sometimes absurdly cheaper. Anything that is mostly a person's hour — a nurse's, a professor's, a babysitter's — outran inflation year after year.

The usual suspects have alibis

Greed? Television makers would also enjoy higher prices. Greed is a constant, and a constant can't explain a 97-percent fall on one side of the chart and a doubling on the other.

Bloat? Hospitals and universities have administrations to point at. A babysitter alone in a living room has none, and her rate climbed anyway.

Worse value? The television improved while its price collapsed. The sitter reads the same books aloud she read in 1999.

The real answer dates to 1965, when the economists William Baumol and William Bowen asked why string quartets kept getting more expensive to stage. A quartet takes the same forty musician-minutes it took in Mozart's day, yet musicians' pay rises, because the people who could be musicians could instead work in industries where an hour produces more every year. Baumol and Bowen worked out what that gap does to prices and called it cost disease. It shapes more of your cost of living than any villain does.

Watch it happen in a town of 24 people

The hard part about seeing cost disease in the wild is everything piled on top of it: insurance, licensing, rent, subsidies. So strip all of that away. Below is a town with exactly two jobs. Software, where a machine can multiply what an engineer ships in an hour. And babysitting, where one sitter watches one kid and an hour is an hour. It's 1999 and both jobs pay $12. You control a single button, and it only ever improves the software side. Press it, and watch the price of the thing you didn't touch.

    Everything the town showed you scales. Childcare, tuition, hospitals, veterinary care, haircuts, live theater, elder care: wherever the product is mostly a human hour, its relative price drifts upward as the rest of the economy speeds up. That is not an acquittal of any particular bill; real industries stack real bloat and real market power on top. But it means a service can grow more expensive forever while nobody does anything wrong, and a budget built on "services shouldn't outrun inflation" loses that bet for as long as productivity keeps rising somewhere else. Some of every doubled bill is simply the price of everyone's hour — including yours — becoming worth more.

    The mechanism is William Baumol and William Bowen's cost disease (1965): when technology multiplies what an hour of work produces in one sector, that hour's value rises everywhere — pulled up through paychecks or through cheaper goods, but either way an unchanged human hour becomes relatively expensive. Everything on this page is a small two-sector economy running live in your browser. For the real thing, start with Baumol & Bowen's On the Performing Arts: The Anatomy of Their Economic Problems (1965), or the modern overview in Revisiting Baumol's Disease (2023).

    What the model assumes (and what it doesn't claim)
    • The whole lesson is one identity: with a shared wage w, a care-hour costs w and a software unit costs w ÷ A, so pcare / psoftware = A — the productivity multiple. Demand is a two-good CES system (the "will families accept an alternative?" lever is its elasticity of substitution), with a floor on care demand because parents work regardless of price.
    • The town is a parable, not a history. Real IDEs did not double output, and "software units" are a fiction; the eras are named for flavor, sized for arithmetic.
    • Studios hire anyone trained, pay what an hour produces, and sell software at labor cost — no profits, no capital owners, no unemployment beyond retraining and unbooked sitters.
    • Workers are interchangeable after retraining, and a sitter's hour is one hour of one-on-one care unless a policy changes the product (batching, the robo-app).
    • The robo-sitter is a stylized routing rule — families who accept it take it when it's cheaper — not a full substitution model. Its price (3 software units/hr) and its thinner attention are assumptions, on display, not findings.
    • The toy isolates one mechanism: the rising opportunity cost of human time. Real childcare, healthcare, and tuition also carry administration, market power, insurance, and quality change — none of which are modeled here.
