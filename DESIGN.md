The irreducible idea is:

> **When technology makes one worker much more productive elsewhere, an hour of ordinary human labor becomes more expensive—even in activities where that hour produces no more than before.**

Baumol’s original structure has a “progressive” sector with rapid productivity growth and a “non-progressive” sector with slower growth. Because the sectors share a labor market, wages in both are pulled upward by productivity in the progressive sector; the stagnant sector’s labor cost per unit consequently rises. ([Intereconomics][1])

## My recommended premise: **The Town of Things and Time**

A small town has two places to work:

* A **gadget factory**, where machines continually multiply the number of gadgets one worker can produce.
* A **care house**, where one hour of attentive care still requires one caregiver to spend one hour with another person.

All townspeople are initially interchangeable workers. The player repeatedly improves the factory.

At the beginning:

* One factory worker makes **one gadget per hour**.
* One caregiver provides **one care-hour per hour**.
* Therefore, one care-hour costs roughly **one gadget**.

After three doublings:

* One factory worker makes **eight gadgets per hour**.
* One caregiver still provides **one care-hour per hour**.
* A caregiver who is paid less than the factory wage leaves for the factory.
* To retain caregivers, the care house must now charge roughly **eight gadgets for one care-hour**.

Nothing deteriorated inside the care house. The same caregiver provides the same care. What changed is the value of the caregiver’s alternative use of time.

That gives you the central visual inscription:

> **Nothing happened to care. Everything happened to its alternative.**

Or, more quantitatively:

> **One care-hour still costs one human hour. A gadget now costs 7.5 human minutes.**

This is the cleanest route to the intuition because it reframes cost disease as **the rising opportunity cost of presence**, rather than as mysterious inflation, bureaucratic bloat, or declining competence.

## Use a trivial tutorial, then reveal the consequential version

I would actually introduce the mechanic with **Hats and Haircuts**, then morph it into **Gadgets and Care** without changing the underlying equations.

### Fifteen-second tutorial: Hats and Haircuts

A hat press doubles its output. A haircut still occupies half an hour. Hairdressers begin leaving for the hat factory, so haircut prices rise in hat-equivalents.

This version has almost no policy baggage. The user immediately understands why a haircut costs more hats.

Then the buildings visually transform:

* Barber shop → eldercare home
* Hairdresser → nurse or caregiver
* Hat press → automated manufacturing plant

The graphs remain unchanged. That transformation communicates that the effect is not peculiar to barbers; it applies wherever **human time is substantially part of the product**.

| **Gadgets and Care**                | Robot factory              | One-on-one care hour                    | Best overall representation of health, childcare and eldercare                 | Needs and quality must be held explicit                           |

### The best public-service version: **Factory and Watchtower**

This is unusually clean because the service output can be defined without hand-waving:

> One staffed watchtower for one hour requires one guard-hour.

The factory becomes more productive and can pay more. The town then has four possible responses:

1. Raise the guard wage and increase taxes.
2. Hold wages down and tolerate vacancies.
3. reduce guard coverage.
4. replace some guarding with cameras or automation.

This makes public-budget dynamics immediately legible. It is less good for illustrating quality, but exceptionally good for illustrating why “just hold the budget constant” results in fewer services.

### The best quality-sensitive version: **Bread and Lessons**

A teacher initially teaches eight children. The player can apparently “increase productivity” by raising the class size to sixteen or thirty-two.

But the toy separately tracks:

* students processed,
* teacher-hours,
* actual learning or attention per student.

This is important because service-sector productivity is often difficult to define precisely: increasing the count of patients treated or students enrolled may not constitute productivity growth if quality deteriorates. Quality measurement is a particularly serious issue in personal services such as health and education. ([Intereconomics][1])

The user should be able to discover that three things commonly called “efficiency” are quite different:

* **Genuine augmentation:** technology lets a teacher maintain quality while serving more students.
* **Batching:** more students receive less individual attention.
* **Product substitution:** an automated lesson replaces a human lesson with a related but different product.

## The causal sequence should be visible, not merely graphed

The animation should show the chain rather than jumping directly from “factory upgrade” to “care price increased.”

### 1. Productivity diverges

The factory visibly accelerates. One worker’s pile changes from:

`1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 gadgets`

The care-house clock continues moving at the same speed.

### 2. Wage pressure propagates

A factory wage sign rises. Care workers acquire little thought bubbles showing the higher factory wage and begin walking toward the factory.

This is the bridge many explanations omit. Without a shared labor market, rising service costs seem arbitrary.

### 3. The service provider responds

The care house raises its wage and price. Workers turn around and return.

The readout should say:

> **Care did not become less efficient. Care had to compete for increasingly valuable workers.**

### 4. Society chooses what gives way

The player then chooses among:

| Response                             | Visible result                                                             |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Pay the higher price**             | Staffing and quality survive; service spending rises                       |
| **Cap prices or wages**              | Workers leave; vacancies and queues grow                                   |
| **Increase caseload**                | Headline throughput rises; quality may fall                                |
| **Substitute technology**            | Cost falls, but the product may change                                     |
| **Buy less service**                 | Sector contracts and access declines                                       |
| **Subsidize or publicly provide it** | Access is preserved; tax burden and distributional conflict become visible |

This makes the visualization a policy toy rather than merely an animated equation.

## The important demand-side wrinkle

The **relative price increase** follows directly from the productivity gap under the simplified assumptions. But the claim that care, education or the arts must eventually consume most employment or spending is not automatic.

It depends on what people do when the service becomes relatively expensive:

* When services and goods are **poor substitutes**, people continue demanding the service and its spending and employment share tends to rise.
* When scalable substitutes are acceptable—recorded music for concerts, automated lessons for tutors—the human service may shrink.
* When the service is strongly income-elastic, increasing prosperity can cause people to buy more of it despite its rising price.

Research on public services similarly finds that the labor and budget shift depends on services being sufficiently poor substitutes for private goods or demand being relatively inelastic. ([Oxford Department of Economics][2])

So the sandbox should distinguish two propositions:

1. **Cost disease:** the service’s relative unit price rises.
2. **Structural transformation:** labor and expenditure move toward the service.

The first comes from production and wages. The second additionally requires a demand assumption.

## A compact model for the implementation

Let:

* (A_G(t)) be gadgets produced per worker-hour.
* (A_S(t)) be service units produced per worker-hour.
* (w(t)) be the shared market wage.
* (p_G(t)) and (p_S(t)) be unit prices.

Then:

[
p_G(t)=\frac{w(t)}{A_G(t)}
]

[
p_S(t)=\frac{w(t)}{A_S(t)}
]

Therefore:

[
\frac{p_S(t)}{p_G(t)}
=====================

\frac{A_G(t)}{A_S(t)}
]

That last equation is essentially the entire tutorial. If gadget productivity grows eightfold while service productivity is unchanged, the service becomes eight times as expensive **relative to gadgets**, irrespective of whether the interface represents this as:

* rising service prices,
* falling gadget prices,
* rising wages,
* or some combination of all three.

### Demand in one additional parameter

A useful advanced control is **substitutability**, (\sigma). In a simple two-good CES model, the ratio of service to goods expenditure behaves like:

[
\frac{E_S}{E_G}
===============

K\left(\frac{p_S}{p_G}\right)^{1-\sigma}
]

Interpretation:

* (\sigma<1): poor substitutes; the service spending share rises.
* (\sigma=1): spending shares remain roughly constant.
* (\sigma>1): users substitute away; the human service share falls.

The public-facing slider need not say “elasticity of substitution.” It could be labeled:

> **Will people accept an alternative?**
> Never ← Sometimes ← Readily

## The most revealing controls

The full sandbox only needs a handful of controls:

### Productivity

* Factory productivity growth
* Service productivity growth
* How much technology augments versus replaces workers

### Labor market

* Ease of changing occupations
* Training delay
* How strongly productivity gains flow into wages versus profits and lower prices

### Demand

* Minimum required service level
* Willingness to substitute
* Income sensitivity: do richer citizens demand more care, education or culture?

### Institutions

* Market pricing
* Public provision
* Wage or price caps
* Subsidies
* Quality standards

### Distribution

* Equal productivity dividend
* Gains concentrated among capital owners or high-wage workers
* Progressive versus flat taxation

That final control matters. In the aggregate, a richer society may remain capable of buying increasingly expensive services. But when income growth is uneven, lower-income households can lose access even as average income rises; recent work on Baumol effects emphasizes this distributional distinction. ([Intereconomics][1])

A powerful display would therefore show two affordability measures side by side:

* **Average household:** care-hours affordable
* **Bottom-quartile household:** care-hours affordable

This prevents “society can afford it” from silently becoming “every individual can afford it.”

## Metrics worth keeping on screen

I would avoid GDP at first. The useful readouts are more tangible:

1. **One service hour costs:** 8 gadgets
2. **Average wage:** 8 gadgets per hour
3. **Workers:** 6 factory / 18 service
4. **Residents receiving service:** 82%
5. **Average wait:** 11 days
6. **Quality:** 73%
7. **Public budget share:** 31%
8. **Material consumption:** 4× its starting level

The last metric produces the characteristic Baumol paradox:

> The service budget looks alarming, yet the town is materially far richer than before.

## What I would not use as the opening premise

I would avoid starting with actual American healthcare or college tuition. Those cases contain administrative overhead, market power, regulation, insurance, subsidies, amenities, signaling and quality changes. Users will reasonably argue about which component is “really” responsible and never internalize the clean mechanism.

Restaurants are relatable but similarly imperfect. BLS data characterize restaurant growth from 1990 through 2019 as predominantly hours-driven with very low productivity growth, but measured restaurant productivity accelerated markedly after 2019—an illustration that real industries can change regimes rather than remaining permanently stagnant. ([Bureau of Labor Statistics][3])

Housing is an especially bad default because land scarcity, zoning and location rents dominate the causal story.

## The strongest overall design

I would build the experience under the title:

# **One Hour Is Still One Hour**

The first screen is **Hats and Haircuts**. After the first successful intuition, it transforms into **Things and Care**. A scenario selector then applies the same engine to:

* education,
* healthcare,
* childcare,
* public safety,
* live performance,
* restaurants,
* and AI-era human verification.

The central interaction is a single large button:

> **Invent a better machine**

Each press makes the factory astonishingly better—and creates a new problem somewhere that did not change at all. That is the Baumol intuition in its most compact, generative form.
